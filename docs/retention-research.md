# Swap Seven Headlines for One Story

Ai News (@thedailyai-01) is losing viewers at the swipe, not in the body: a **29% "stayed to watch" rate means 71 of every 100 people who see the Short in the feed leave inside the first one to three seconds**, before the 90-second, seven-story structure has even started to matter. Real dashboards from Shorts that broke out show **74–84% viewed / 15–25% swiped away** ([vidIQ](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/why-your-shorts-aren-t-getting-views-any-more)), and the one published small-channel example at 26.6% viewed is treated by its author as a problem to fix, not a baseline ([Social Media Examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/how-to-analyze-your-youtube-shorts/)). Across every source reviewed, the short-form AI-news formats that actually produce views are **single-story, 45–90 seconds, with a human or character hook in the first 3–5 seconds and screen capture or B-roll for the rest**, posted 2–4 times a week; no faceless daily roundup Shorts account with strong per-video numbers surfaced anywhere, and the one same-channel comparison available shows a roundup-titled upload earning less than half of a single-release upload ([creatordb](https://creatordb.app/creatorstats/mreflow/)). The recommended redesign therefore has two parts, in priority order: first, rebuild the opening so that frame one is a readable headline about one named company or model with the stakes spoken by second three, and delete any branded intro; second, cut the format from seven stories in 90 seconds to one story in 30–45 seconds (with a three-story "top three" variant at 55–58 seconds as the comparison arm), because at 140 words per minute a seven-story Short gives each story roughly 25 words, below the two-sentence minimum every pacing guideline implies. Length and hook data from large view samples, YouTube's own metric definitions, and the observed formats of the top channels are reasonably solid; nearly every specific retention percentage in circulation (hook retention thresholds, caption lifts, cut-rate effects) comes from tool vendors without disclosed methodology and is flagged as such throughout. The channel's own Studio analytics, baselined over the next 20 Shorts, should override any external number.

## The 29% figure is a hook failure, measured by the metric YouTube actually ranks on

YouTube exposes three Shorts-specific signals: "Shown in feed" (impressions), "Viewed vs. swiped away" (the share of impressions where the viewer stayed rather than flicking past), and, since 31 March 2025, "Engaged views," defined as views where "a viewer chooses to continue watching beyond the initial playback" ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-algorithm/)). Under the March 2025 rule change, "any YouTube Short that starts playing or replays will be counted as a view, with no minimum watch time required," which means the raw view count on the channel's 30–200-view Shorts now includes every swipe-past that briefly autoplayed; only engaged views count toward Partner Program eligibility and revenue ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-algorithm/); [Shortimize](https://www.shortimize.com/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-rate)). The channel's "stayed to watch" number is therefore the swipe decision itself, which YouTube's Shorts team describes as being made in about **one second** ([YouTube Official Blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/)). This is evidence-backed: it follows from YouTube's own metric definitions, not from any vendor.

YouTube publishes no numeric threshold for a "good" viewed rate; its discovery page says ranking uses whether viewers "choose to watch it, ignore it, or click 'not interested'," plus average view duration, average percentage viewed, likes and post-watch surveys, and the system "doesn't favor any particular format" ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225)). The numbers that exist come from creator dashboards and aggregators. vidIQ showed a 1.3M-view Short at **84% viewed / 15% swiped** with roughly 90% average percentage viewed, and a second at 74% / 25% with 93% watch-through, while warning these figures "don't guarantee success" ([vidIQ](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/why-your-shorts-aren-t-getting-views-any-more)). Subscribr's guidance is to "aim for 75–80% viewed versus 20–25% swiped away" and treat ~80% average percentage viewed as strong for sub-60-second Shorts ([Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/youtube-shorts-analytics-metrics-growth)). Vendor aggregates put average percentage viewed for Shorts around **73%** overall, with length-tiered targets (over 60% for under 15 s, over 50% for 15–30 s, over 40% for 30–60 s) that the authors themselves describe as aggregator conventions rather than YouTube thresholds ([GoFaceless](https://www.gofaceless.ai/en/blog/high-retention-youtube-shorts-attributes)). Paddy Galloway's 3.3-billion-view analysis, as presented by vidIQ in March 2026, found Shorts that keep at least **60% of viewers past the opening seconds** cluster among viral outliers and frames the hook as the one lever creators control ([vidIQ via Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/creator-economy/video/this-is-why-your-shorts-fail-d9584254-4b96-4ba2-8769-314731b083ae/)); that 60% figure is the single most repeated threshold across sources, though the page is ambiguous about whether it measures "past the hook" or "watched the whole clip."

The practical diagnostic, stated by both Social Media Examiner and Bytecap, is a two-by-two: low viewed percentage with high average percentage viewed means the hook is broken and the body is fine; high viewed with low average percentage viewed means the hook works and the body or pacing fails ([Social Media Examiner](https://www.socialmediaexaminer.com/how-to-analyze-your-youtube-shorts/); [Bytecap](https://www.bytecap.io/research/youtube-shorts-retention-benchmarks)). Ai News sits firmly in the first quadrant on the one number available. The inference that follows is important for sequencing the redesign: shortening the video will raise average percentage viewed and engaged views, but it will not by itself move the 29%, because most of those 71 swipers never experienced the length. Frame one, the on-screen headline, and the first spoken line have to change first. The seed-audience mechanism Todd Sherman described, where YouTube tests each Short on a small group and widens distribution only if that group engages, means a 29% viewed rate also caps impressions, which is consistent with the 30–200-view plateau ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-algorithm/)).

## Seven stories in 90 seconds violates every pacing and depth rule found

The large-sample length data is older than ideal but points the same way from two directions. Nate Black's analysis of 35 billion Shorts views found "both 13 seconds and 60 seconds were the two most cumulative views" lengths, and that breakout channels had published at least 200 Shorts ([vidIQ panel, 2023](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/let-s-talk-about-youtube-shorts)). Galloway and Gileta's 33-channel, 5,400-Short dataset found most creators make 20–40-second Shorts, that **50–60 seconds "maximises average view duration,"** and that Shorts holding viewers past 50 seconds averaged 4.1M views ([VideoGen summary](https://videogen.io/blog/decoding-the-youtube-shorts-algorithm-a-deep-dive-into-3-3-billion-views)). Jenny Hoyos, then averaging about 10M views per Short, reported her top performers averaged **34 seconds** and framed the stakes memorably: "on a Short, one second is over 1% retention" ([vidIQ interview](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/how-jenny-hoyos-actually-makes-viral-shorts)). Vendor guidance converges on a narrower band: OpusClip claims 15–30-second Shorts "often exceed 80%" retention, that educational content fits 35–45 seconds "if payoff is clear," and that "retention drops significantly after 45 s for most content," all without disclosed data ([OpusClip](https://opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention)). There is **no retrievable performance data at all for Shorts between 60 seconds and the 3-minute cap**, and the closest platform statement is Instagram's reported internal slide that Reels over 90 seconds "can actually negatively impact creators' reach" ([TechBriefly](https://techbriefly.com/2024/05/20/instagrams-secret-criteria-for-reels-90-seconds)). The 90-second Ai News format thus sits at the far edge of the one length band nobody has measured and at the precise point where a sister platform says reach falls.

The story-count problem is arithmetic as much as evidence. Publisher data-news Shorts in a 50-video sample (BBC, ITV, Guardian, Times, FT) averaged just under 84 seconds at roughly 100–150 words per minute, which the author translated into **~200-word scripts** covering a single topic each, with "causes and effects left for longer formats" ([Online Journalism Blog](https://onlinejournalismblog.com/2026/08/10/how-data-journalism-is-done-on-tiktok-six-key-takeaways-from-watching-50-videos/)). A 90-second Ai News episode at 140 wpm holds about 210 words; after a hook and sign-off, seven stories get roughly **25 words each**, under two sentences. OpusClip's pacing rules call for a new visual or story beat every 5–7 seconds and no single element on screen longer than 8 seconds, and state that three-step structures "hit the sweet spot" while more than five steps "suggests a long-form video instead" ([OpusClip](https://opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention)); a seven-item Short breaches the step ceiling by definition. Reuters Institute's 2025 Digital News Report, the largest audience study available (94,943 respondents, 48 markets), quotes a young listener praising a show because "it is short and gives the top three things," and finds younger groups want "visual, playful formats and lighter tones" ([Reuters Institute DNR 2025](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary)). That is the only direct audience statement on roundup size found, and it says three, not seven.

No head-to-head retention test of one versus three versus seven stories exists in any source; that comparison is an inference assembled from format-length mappings (single tip or punchline 15–30 s; multi-step or educational 31–60 s) and the watch-time argument that a 40-second Short at 60% retention (24 seconds watched) beats a 20-second Short at 70% (14 seconds) ([OpusClip TikTok](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-length-format-retention-data)). Mario Joos, formerly of MrBeast's organisation, adds the content-quality filter that applies with special force to automated roundups: "a generic tip list loses viewers, while specific, new information builds curiosity," and his test for any segment is whether "another smart person [could] replicate this content without research or thinking" ([Vidpros](https://vidpros.com/insiders/mario-joos-youtube-retention-secrets/)). A 25-word headline summary fails that test every time; a 30–45-second single story with one concrete number and one shown result can pass it.

## The first second must be a headline about one named thing, and the branded intro has to go

Every source agrees on the decision window and almost none can rank hook types. YouTube's own Shorts team member set the bar at hooking viewers in "one second" with a shock-intrigue-satisfy formula ([YouTube Official Blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/)); vidIQ's dashboard walkthrough put the swipe decision at about one second and advised being "in the middle of the action within 3 seconds" ([vidIQ](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/why-your-shorts-aren-t-getting-views-any-more)); Galloway's analysis recommends a 3–5-second hook that lands within the first five seconds ([vidIQ via Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/creator-economy/video/this-is-why-your-shorts-fail-d9584254-4b96-4ba2-8769-314731b083ae/)). Hoyos' structural rules are the most specific creator guidance with a track record attached: hook of no more than 3 seconds, a first frame that works on mute and could serve as a thumbnail, no more than three objects in frame, and a **foreshadowed payoff** ("here are three reasons why X" signals a three-point ending) ([vidIQ interview](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/how-jenny-hoyos-actually-makes-viral-shorts)). The vendor figure that "50–60% of viewers who drop off do so within the first three seconds" is attributed to "data from thousands of Shorts" with no method and should be quoted only as directional ([OpusClip](https://opus.pro/blog/ideal-youtube-shorts-length-format-retention)). The same applies to a dev.to author's self-reported 0.84 correlation between three-second retention and overall performance across 120 videos, and to the claim that curiosity-gap hooks averaged 68% three-second retention ([dev.to Hook Engineering](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9)).

What the archetype lists do establish is the shape of a usable AI-news hook. OpusClip's named formulas with verbatim examples include the direct promise ("This 5-second edit doubled my watch time" rather than "Learn about editing"), the non-yes/no question ("Why do your Shorts die at 40% watch time?"), the numbered promise ("3 settings that fix blurry Shorts"), the pattern interrupt, and the combination ("Longer Shorts actually get more views, and I'll prove it in 30 seconds"), with "Hey guys, welcome back to my channel" as the pattern to avoid; the hook should be complete by 2.5 seconds, spoken text on screen for at least 2 seconds, and an optional 0.3-second flash of the end result can precede the cut back ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-shorts-hook-formulas)). The "nobody talks about" template exists as a named curiosity formula with the constraint of a spoken hook under 15 words and on-screen hook under 10 ([WEDGE Method](https://dev.to/wedgemethoddev/i-analyzed-500-viral-tiktok-videos-these-7-hook-patterns-always-work-331f)). Newsroom practice on TikTok, from a March 2026 study of 11 newsrooms, favours "loop-and-flashback" narratives that "open with dynamic moments before giving context," and warns that audiences respond negatively to "highly polished content" seen as inauthentic ([RJI](https://rjionline.org/news/new-research-reveals-best-practices-for-newsrooms-on-tiktok/)). Real newsroom openers look like the Guardian's "Three years into this war, the biggest threat to people isn't just the fighting" ([Online Journalism Blog](https://onlinejournalismblog.com/2026/08/10/how-data-journalism-is-done-on-tiktok-six-key-takeaways-from-watching-50-videos/)). The inference for Ai News is that the native hook is proof-first and entity-first: "OpenAI just made X free" or "Google's new model does Y, and here's what it costs," with the company logo and a three-to-six-word headline in frame one so the promise reads on mute; sound-off viewing claims range from over 60% on mobile to 54–68% starting muted, all vendor-stated ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-shorts-hook-formulas); [dev.to](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9)). No source shows a question beating a claim or a number; the shared property is specificity plus immediate context, which is why the A/B plan below tests hook *subject* (the biggest story) rather than hook grammar.

On branded intros and the mascot, there is no direct measurement either way, but the indirect case against any pre-content sting is strong: the swipe window is one to three seconds, Shortimize's rule is "skip greetings and context dumps; start mid-action," and Hoyos attributes roughly 90% of her returning viewers to recognisable content "buckets" rather than to intros ([Shortimize](https://www.shortimize.com/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-rate); [vidIQ interview](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/how-jenny-hoyos-actually-makes-viral-shorts)). The practical inference for Byte, the channel's single mascot, is that the character is net-positive only when it *is* the hook, on screen reacting to the headline in frame one, and net-negative as an opening card. Rowan Cheung's format, the one documented AI-news short-form playbook with million-view results, uses the avatar for exactly the first 3–5 seconds "to establish a human connection" and then runs roughly 98% B-roll, screenshots, headlines and animations ([Stormy.ai](https://stormy.ai/blog/ai-avatar-short-form-video-rowan-cheung-playbook), a vendor account with commercial interest). That is a template Byte can occupy.

## Foreshadow the payoff; never say "wait for it"

Open loops are universally recommended and nowhere measured. No source provides lift data for a spoken tease, a countdown or a progress bar on Shorts, TikTok or Reels. What the sources do agree on is the direction of the trade-off. OpusClip explicitly discourages asking viewers to "wait for it" in the hook and burying the main point, claiming payoffs inside the first 15 seconds keep "about 20% more retention" (unsourced), while endorsing mid-video teasers of a specific upcoming item such as on-screen "Wait for tip #3" ([OpusClip TikTok](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-length-format-retention-data)). Both OpusClip and a dev.to author warn that a curiosity gap left unclosed within the Short "will damage trust," and that hooks running past three seconds without a payoff show steep drop-off ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-shorts-hook-formulas); [dev.to](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9)). Shortimize recommends a micro open loop every 5–8 seconds, ending at the payoff, and names the backfire condition: a long setup for a short payoff ([Shortimize](https://www.shortimize.com/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-rate)). Hoyos' "but/so" chaining (set up, resolve, new conflict) and her observation that viewers remember the peak and the ending come closest to a tested method, since they come from a creator with public results ([vidIQ interview](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/how-jenny-hoyos-actually-makes-viral-shorts)).

The defensible version of "the last one is free" for an AI-news Short is therefore inference, built from endorsed patterns: name the payoff category in the hook so the hook is answered immediately ("three AI updates, and one of them is a free tool that does X"), tease it once mid-video with a specific label ("the free one is next"), and deliver it before the final five seconds rather than at the last frame. Ending on the tool also doubles as the loop point. Looping remains a real but changed lever: replays count as views with no minimum watch time since March 2025, but engaged views exclude loops, so a seamless loop inflates vanity views and can push average percentage viewed above 100% without moving the metric that drives monetisation ([vidIQ looping](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/double-youtube-watch-time-looping-shorts/); [GoFaceless](https://www.gofaceless.ai/en/blog/high-retention-youtube-shorts-attributes)). CTA guidance is consistent and unquantified: no outro, since interactive end screens do not exist under 60 seconds; the CTA lives in the final 3–5 seconds as lower-third text of five to seven words; "Follow for Part 2" or "I post this every day" outperform "like and follow" in every vendor's opinion; and the only external path is a pinned comment, where YouTube's 2025 creator report reportedly measured a **1.5% link click-through** ([FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/call-to-action-youtube-shorts); [Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/scripting-youtube-shorts-engagement)). Shorts-to-long-form conversion runs 0.2–0.5% by Joos' account, which means each Short has to be worth watching on its own rather than serve as a trailer ([Vidpros](https://vidpros.com/insiders/mario-joos-youtube-retention-secrets/)).

Three patterns matter for Ai News. First, **every documented high-view AI-news Short is built around one proper noun or one concrete capability** (Zuckerberg, 1X Neo, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5, "AI Video Researcher Agent"); "N things happened today" titles are absent from every top list collected, and count-led titles only win when the count promises usable tips rather than headlines. Second, the faceless roundup formats that do have audiences (AI Revolution at 84K average views) are 17-minute long-form with a narrator, and no faceless daily-roundup Shorts account with strong per-video numbers surfaced in any source; the one autopilot roundup channel found (AILoop) and a creator who posted an AI-generated Short daily for three months, saw 15–100 views per Short, then collapsed to 0–2 views because the videos "looked too similar" after four or five uploads, are the closest analogues to the current Ai News format ([Nikolay100](https://nikolay100.com/en/uploaded-youtube-shorts-every-day-for-3-months/), pre-2025). Third, cadence does not rescue format: the highest-frequency account in the data (Hassid, 9.3 posts per week) has the weakest per-post views, while the million-view accounts post two to four times a week. YouTube's Shorts lead has said there is no hidden threshold where five or ten uploads per day unlocks reach, "nothing in our systems" like that, and that for news-focused channels responsiveness to current events matters more than time of day ([Benzinga](https://benzinga.com/z/34020532)).

The policy backdrop reinforces the same direction. YouTube's "inauthentic content" guideline, effective 15 July 2025, makes mass-produced or repetitive uploads ineligible for monetisation, with "slideshows with identical narration across videos" given as an example, while stating AI use alone is not a violation ([Netinfluencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/youtube-clarifies-july-15-policy-update-repetitious-content-rebranded-as-inauthentic-content/)). YouTube's 2026 priorities blog commits to reducing "the spread of low quality, repetitive content," notes that for sensitive topics including news a Gen-AI label may appear on the video itself, and reports that over a million channels used YouTube's AI creation tools daily in December 2025 ([Android Central](https://www.androidcentral.com/apps-software/youtube/youtube-will-help-creators-use-ai-in-2026-as-long-as-its-not-slop)). The Next Web reported 16 channel terminations under the policy in January 2026 and a viewer "AI slop" rating pop-up tested from March 2026, with enforcement described as channel-level across the last 30 uploads ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/youtube-ai-slop-crackdown-faceless-creators-collateral-damage)). Non-photorealistic card animation with a synthetic voice is unlikely to trigger the automatic photorealism label introduced in May 2026 ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/05/27/youtube-generative-ai-labels-easier-to-read-apply/)), but 30 near-identical daily episodes is precisely the pattern the policy names. Varying the script structure, visuals and headline per episode is therefore a distribution requirement as well as a creative one.

## Titles serve search weeks later; the feed only sees frame one

Packaging advice has to start from a stark platform fact: a YouTube Shorts team member said in January 2025 that "99.9% of the views are coming from the Shorts feed," where no thumbnail appears, and Creator Liaison Rene Ritchie repeated in July 2026 that most Shorts are still watched in the feed even after custom Shorts thumbnails launched for Partner Program channels ([YouTube Official Blog](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-shorts-deep-dive/); [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/youtube-ends-2-year-wait-for-shorts-thumbnails-but-blocks-a-b-testing/)). The title is not a click decision in the feed; its job is search and browse indexing later and the channel grid. Socialinsider's 2025 brand data shows average views per Short by window: days 1–5 at 833, days 5–10 at 1,786, then a dip, then **3,310 in days 25–30**, which the authors attribute to search and browse discovery ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/how-long-does-it-take-for-yt-shorts-to-get-views/)). A title like "5 AI stories today" has no searchable entity and cannot catch that second wave; "OpenAI makes Sora free for everyone | AI News 4 Nov" can, and the series tag after the pipe preserves the channel-grid identity. This is inference from the traffic curve, since no study has compared story-led and count-led Shorts titles for search pickup, and YouTube's title A/B tool applies to long-form only while Shorts thumbnails explicitly cannot be A/B tested ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-title-a-b-testing-rolls-out-globally-to-creators); [ppc.land](https://ppc.land/youtube-ends-2-year-wait-for-shorts-thumbnails-but-blocks-a-b-testing/)).

The cover frame and hashtags follow from the same logic. Custom Shorts thumbnails appear on the homepage and the channel's Home and Shorts tabs only, and because the 9:16 frame is cropped to roughly 3:2 on some surfaces, the headline must sit in the central safe area ([ppc.land](https://ppc.land/youtube-ends-2-year-wait-for-shorts-thumbnails-but-blocks-a-b-testing/)); making frame one a large-type headline card with one logo lets the same frame double as cover. Hashtags are optional: YouTube Help says up to three appear above the title, more than 60 causes all to be ignored, and they help viewers find related content through search ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6390658)); Sherman's guidance is that hashtags help when "associated with a real world thing" such as an event or product ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/25/youtube-demystifies-the-shorts-algorithm-views-and-answers-other-creator-questions/)), which favours #OpenAI or #Gemini over #ai #shorts #news. Description links are not clickable in the Shorts player; a plain-text source list is still worth including for trust and because news is a sensitive category under YouTube's labelling rules.

Posting-time data is contradictory and should not drive the schedule. Buffer's 1.8M-video dataset favours Friday 4–7 pm and weekday evenings 6–11 pm, with Tuesday and Monday weakest ([Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/best-time-to-post-on-youtube/)); Adobe Express's 24,000-Short study favours Tuesday and Saturday 4 pm and reports "off-peak" posts earning about 40% more views than the crowded 8–10 am slot ([ContentGrip summary](https://www.contentgrip.com/best-times-to-post-youtube-shorts/)); Hootsuite, via OpenClip, favours weekday 12–3 pm ([OpenClip](https://openclip.app/guides/best-time-to-post-shorts)). None names a time zone and all are brand-account data. The one consistent signal is that the 8–10 am slot underperforms, and the one platform statement is that freshness matters more than hour for news ([Benzinga](https://benzinga.com/z/34020532)). Mario Joos' working theory, unconfirmed by YouTube, is that since mid-September 2025 the feed deprioritises Shorts older than about 28–30 days ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-shorts-algorithm-may-now-favor-fresh-over-evergreen/562661/)); if real, this favours dated news that never competes with its own back catalogue. Cross-posting is independent reach rather than a YouTube lever, and Socialinsider's 2025–2026 data shows Shorts delivering **15,160 average views for brand accounts under 5K followers versus 625 on Reels and 350 on TikTok**, with TikTok leading on engagement rate ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/tiktok-vs-instagram-reels-vs-youtube-shorts/)); post clean, unwatermarked exports everywhere and keep YouTube as the master.

## The recommended format: one story in 45 seconds, tested against a "top three" at 55

The redesign keeps the chosen card-stack "Distilled" look, cream palette and Byte, and changes what happens in the first three seconds, how many stories fit, and how long the video runs. The two skeletons below are synthesised from the cited timings (Subscribr's 0–3 / 3–15 / 15–50 / 50–60 structure, OpusClip's hook-by-2.5 s and quick-win-by-10 s rules, FluxNote's final 3–5-second CTA window) and are inference, not a template any top channel has published ([Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/scripting-youtube-shorts-engagement); [OpusClip TikTok](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-length-format-retention-data); [FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/call-to-action-youtube-shorts)). Both drop the branded intro entirely, move Byte into the hook as the reacting character, animate every card so nothing sits static beyond 5 seconds, and keep bold word-or-phrase captions out of the lower UI zone. At roughly 140 words per minute, Format A holds about 100 words and Format B about 130.

**Format A: single story, 40–45 seconds (primary arm)**

| Time | Beat | What is on screen | Script guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0–2.5 s | Hook, proof-first | Headline card, 3–6 words, one company logo, Byte reacting; readable on mute | "[Company] just [did Y], and it's free." Spoken hook under 15 words; no greeting, no sting. Optional 0.3-s flash of the result before cutting back. |
| 2.5–8 s | Why it matters | Card 2: one stat or one comparison, animated reveal | One sentence that delivers the quick win before 10 s: who this changes things for and by how much. |
| 8–22 s | What it does | 5–8 s screen capture or product clip (the "result" shown), then one card with the concrete limit or number | Two beats maximum; one "but" at ~15 s to open a micro-loop ("But there's a catch…"). |
| 22–34 s | The catch or the context | Card 3: price, availability, competitor comparison | Resolve the "but"; one specific number. Comment prompt embedded here, not after the payoff ("Would you switch? Tell me below"). |
| 34–41 s | How to get it today | Card 4 with "Link in pinned comment" lower-third | Actionable step; this is the payoff and the natural loop point. |
| 41–44 s | Cadence CTA | Final frame held 1–2 s, five-to-seven-word lower third | "I do this every day. Follow so you don't miss tomorrow's." Line echoes the hook; hard cut, no outro. |

**Format B: "top three things," 55–58 seconds (comparison arm)**

| Time | Beat | What is on screen | Script guidance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0–2.5 s | Hook naming the payoff | Three-logo headline card, Byte reacting | "Three AI updates today, and the last one is a free tool that [specific benefit]." The hook is answered immediately; never a bare "wait for it." |
| 2.5–17 s | Story 1 (biggest) | Two animated cards, visual change every 3–5 s | Two sentences, ~30 words: what happened, one number. |
| 17–19 s | Teaser of a specific next item | Transition card | "Number two is weirder." |
| 19–33 s | Story 2 | Two cards | Two sentences, ~30 words. |
| 33–35 s | Mid-video retention hook at the 30-s mark | Transition card | "The free one is next." |
| 35–50 s | Story 3, the tool | 5–8 s screen demo, then cost and how to get it; "Link in pinned comment" during, not after | Shown, not described. |
| 50–55 s | Cadence CTA | Final frame held 1–2 s | Five to seven words; hard cut. |

The title formula is story-led with a series suffix: **"[Company or model] [active verb] [concrete object or number] | AI News [date]"**, under 60 characters before the pipe, named entity first, and never a count of headlines (for Format B, lead with the biggest story and let the suffix carry the roundup identity: "OpenAI makes Sora free, plus 2 more | AI News 4 Nov"). The description leads with the same sentence, lists sources as plain text, declares the AI voice and visuals in one line, and carries three event-specific hashtags (#OpenAI, #Sora, #AINews). The cover frame is frame one of the video, headline in the central 3:2 safe area.

The posting plan is one Short per day, every day, at a fixed slot around **13:00–15:00 UTC** (US morning, EU afternoon, South Asia evening), chosen because it lands after the US-morning news cycle and avoids the one slot every dataset agrees is crowded, not because any hour data is trustworthy; Studio's "When your viewers are on YouTube" report replaces this once the channel has 20 Shorts of data. The autopilot should pick the day's single biggest story by a concreteness rule (a named product, a price, a free tier, a shown result beats a funding round or an executive quote) and should vary the card sequence, transition lines and headline structure per episode so that the last 30 uploads do not read as a template. A second daily Short is justified only when a second story is genuinely distinct, never as a re-cut of the same episode.

The two-week A/B plan has to be sequential, because YouTube offers no Shorts packaging A/B tooling, and it is deliberately underpowered relative to Shortimize's suggestion of 20 Shorts for a baseline and 8–12 per test arm ([Shortimize](https://www.shortimize.com/blog/youtube-shorts-retention-rate)); treat the end of week two as a checkpoint, not a verdict, and extend to three weeks if arms are within ten points of each other.

| Day | Arm | Fixed across arms | Primary read-out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days 1–14, alternating (odd days A, even days B) | Format A single story (7 Shorts) vs Format B top three (7 Shorts) | New hook on every episode: no intro, headline card in frame one, Byte as reactor, spoken stakes by 3 s; story-led title; 13:00–15:00 UTC slot | Viewed vs swiped away (hook health); average percentage viewed compared only within length cohort (30–45 s vs 46–60 s); engaged views; subscribers per 1,000 views |
| Day 7 | Interim check | – | If either arm's viewed rate is still under 45%, inspect frame one and the first spoken line before touching anything else; the body is not yet the problem |
| Days 8–14 | Within-arm hook subject test | Format fixed per day as above | Alternate the hook subject between "named product or model" and "free tool or price change" to learn which story type lowers swipe-away for this audience |
| Day 14 and day 30 | Decision and second look | – | Pick the arm with the higher viewed rate and the higher engaged-view share; re-read day-30 search and browse traffic in Studio, since the Socialinsider curve puts the largest view window at days 25–30 |

The pre-registered milestones, in order, are: viewed vs swiped from 29% to **at least 50%** (the "viewed exceeds swiped" bar from Social Media Examiner), then toward the 70–80% band seen on breakout Shorts; average percentage viewed at or above 70% for Format A and 60% for Format B, consistent with length-tiered vendor targets; and a visible rise in engaged-view share, the metric that gates monetisation. Expect Shorts to look dead for the first five to ten days and judge at day 14 and day 30, not day 2 ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/how-long-does-it-take-for-yt-shorts-to-get-views/)).

The evidence grade for each recommendation, so the channel knows what it is betting on:

| Recommendation | Grade | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Fix the hook before the length; 29% viewed is a swipe-window failure | Evidence-backed | YouTube metric definitions; YouTube staff "one second" statement; creator dashboards at 74–84% viewed |
| Drop the branded intro; frame one is the headline | Strongly inferred | No direct test; every hook rule and the 1–3-s window imply it |
| One story (or at most three) per Short; abandon seven | Evidence-backed by observation, not by a controlled test | Every high-view AI short is single-story; same-channel roundup underperformed; step-ceiling and words-per-story arithmetic; DNR "top three" audience quote |
| 30–45 s single story; 55–58 s for three stories; never 90 s | Mixed | Large-sample 2023 view data (13 s and 60 s peaks; 50–60 s maximises AVD) is solid but old; the 15–45-s retention bands are vendor claims; no data exists for 60–180 s |
| Foreshadow the payoff, tease a specific item mid-video, no "wait for it" | Inference from consistent but unmeasured guidance | OpusClip, Shortimize, Hoyos agree; zero lift data for any device |
| Story-led, entity-first titles with a series suffix | Inference | 99.9%-feed statistic plus days 25–30 search wave; no title A/B data for Shorts |
| Posting slot 13:00–15:00 UTC | Weak | Three vendor datasets conflict; only "avoid 8–10 am" and "freshness beats hour" are consistent |
| Vary episodes to avoid the inauthentic-content pattern | Evidence-backed on policy, inferred on reach | Policy text is explicit; reach effects are anecdotal |
| Byte as the hook reactor, not an intro element | Inference | Rowan Cheung's 3–5-s avatar hook is the only documented analogue, from a vendor source |
| Caption lifts, cut-rate percentages, "50–60% drop in 3 s" | Do not quote as fact | All vendor-stated with no disclosed method |

## Conclusion

The research reframes the channel's problem from "people don't finish 90-second videos" to "people don't start them," and that reframing changes the order of work: the next 14 uploads should be judged almost entirely on the viewed-versus-swiped number, which the autopilot can move by changing roughly three seconds of each video, before any argument about length is settled. The deeper implication is that the "daily AI news roundup" category, as a faceless Short, has no documented winners at all; what wins is a daily *single* AI story told like a product demo, with a recognisable character for three seconds and a shown result for the rest, which is a format the existing pipeline can produce with less content per episode, not more.

The second implication cuts against the autopilot's instinct toward uniformity. The sources that bear on automated channels, from YouTube's inauthentic-content policy to the creator whose daily AI Shorts fell to zero views once they "looked too similar," all say the same thing: a consistent look is an asset on the channel grid and a liability in the feed if the structure, lines and visuals repeat. Building variation into the generator, so that each episode differs in headline grammar, card order and transition copy, is as much a retention lever as the hook, and it is the one the available evidence suggests most automated news channels never pull.
